What you are actually looking at

Most explanations of netcode are a diagram with arrows. This is the real thing running twice. The page holds two entirely separate game clients. Each one has its own copy of the match, its own input history, its own predictions, and no privileged knowledge of what the other is doing. Everything they know about each other arrives as packets over a simulated link, and that link is yours to abuse: turn latency up, add jitter until packets overtake each other, drop one in five and see what survives.

Because both clients are on screen at once, you can see the thing that is normally invisible. In rollback mode the two views drift apart for a few frames and then snap back into agreement, and the state check stays green a handful of frames behind the present. In naive mode they are already disagreeing on frame one, and by the time you notice they are playing different matches with different scores. That divergence is what a desync actually is, and it is why "just send the positions" quietly stops working the moment two players disagree about anything.

Delay-based netcode in one paragraph

The safest way to keep two clients identical is to never let either of them guess. Delay-based netcode holds your own input back by a few frames before applying it, which gives your opponent's input time to arrive, so every frame is simulated once with both real inputs and the two machines can never disagree. The price is that the delay is permanent and it is felt by both players even on a good connection, and if a single packet arrives late the game has nothing to simulate and has to freeze until it turns up. Set the mode to delay-based, put latency at 90 ms with input delay at 1 frame, and watch the frozen frame counter climb.

Rollback netcode in one paragraph

Rollback refuses to wait. It applies your input immediately and, for the opponent, it guesses: assume they are still holding whatever they held last frame, which for a human on a 60 Hz timeline is right most of the time. The client saves a snapshot of the game state every frame. When the opponent's real input finally arrives for a frame that has already been simulated, the client compares it against the guess it used. If they match, nothing happens at all. If they differ, the client restores the snapshot from that frame, re-simulates every frame from there to the present with the corrected input, and shows the result on the very next frame. Your own controls never lag, and the correction is usually so small that all you see is the opponent shifting slightly.

The loop, step by step

  1. Sample your local input for this frame and store it in a ring buffer.
  2. Send it to the peer, along with the last few inputs you already sent, so one lost packet does not cost anything.
  3. Read whatever inputs have arrived. If any of them contradicts a guess you already used, remember the earliest frame that went wrong.
  4. If there is such a frame, load the snapshot saved before it and re-simulate forward to the present with the corrected inputs.
  5. Snapshot the current state, then simulate one new frame, using the peer's real input if you have it and their last known input if you do not.
  6. Draw. If you are running too far ahead of what the peer has confirmed, skip a frame instead so the rollback distance stays bounded.

Why guessing works

The prediction here is deliberately dumb: repeat the last input. Watch the hit rate counter and against these two bots it sits in the mid eighties, and a human is easier to predict than that, because people hold a direction for many frames at a time. A player who holds a direction for 8 frames only produces two frames where the input actually changes, and only those two frames can possibly cause a rollback. That is the whole reason rollback is cheap enough to ship: you are not predicting the future of the game, you are predicting a value that stays the same most of the time.

The bit nobody draws in the diagram

Rollback has a limit, and the limit is what makes a 200 ms match feel different from a 60 ms one even with perfect rollback. A client can only run so far ahead of what the peer has confirmed before the rollbacks would get longer than its snapshot history, so past that point it stops and waits instead. That is the max rollback slider. If you set latency to 180 ms while max rollback is 8 frames, the peer's input is arriving about 11 frames late, the limit is hit constantly, and the frozen frame counter climbs even in rollback mode. Push max rollback to 14 and the freezing disappears, at the cost of longer re-simulations. Real implementations pick a number around 7 or 8 and accept the hitching beyond it.

The other unglamorous detail is retransmission. Every packet here carries the last few inputs rather than just the newest one, and it also carries an acknowledgement of the newest input received from the peer, so the sender can stretch its window back over anything that has not been acknowledged yet. Turn packet loss up to 25 percent and the match keeps running. Without that, one unlucky packet stalls a lockstep match forever.

What rollback costs you

Correctness comes out of the CPU budget. The sim cost counter shows how many times the game step actually runs per frame displayed. On a clean local connection that number is 1.0. Push latency to 150 ms and it climbs, because every mispredicted input drags the client back several frames and makes it re-run all of them. This is the real design constraint in a rollback game: the simulation step has to be fast and it has to be fully snapshot-able, which usually means a fixed timestep, a state you can copy in one memcpy, and no hidden state in your renderer, your audio, or your animation system.

Is the simulation really deterministic?

It has to be, or the whole thing falls apart. Re-simulating frames 100 through 106 has to produce bit-identical results to the first time they ran, on both machines, or the two clients drift. So the game step in this page uses 32-bit integers only, with positions stored at a 1/64 pixel fixed-point scale, an integer square root written out by hand for the collision normals, and no floating point anywhere in the update. The state is 16 integers, which is what makes a snapshot per frame free.

To prove it rather than claim it, both clients hash their state every frame with FNV-1a and the page compares the two hashes for every frame that both clients have confirmed. The state check panel shows the newest frame that was verified identical on both sides. Switch to naive mode and watch how quickly that guarantee dies. I wrote more about the determinism traps in the browser specifically in determinism in browser games.

Things worth trying

  • Rollback at 180 ms with max rollback at 14. Your own movement stays crisp while the orange player visibly corrects. That asymmetry is the whole point.
  • The same thing with max rollback back down at 8. The corrections stop being free and the frozen frame counter starts moving, because the client keeps hitting its own limit.
  • Delay-based at 180 ms with input delay 1. It becomes unplayable through freezing, not through lag.
  • Delay-based at 180 ms with input delay 8. The freezing stops and the sluggishness begins.
  • Naive at any latency. The state check goes red immediately and the two scoreboards eventually disagree.
  • Packet loss at 25 percent with inputs per packet set to 1, then set it back to 8. Redundant input packets are the cheapest reliability you will ever buy.
  • Jitter at 60 ms. Packets arrive out of order, which is exactly why inputs are addressed by frame number rather than trusted to arrive in sequence.
What is rollback netcode, in short?

It runs the game forward immediately using a guess for the other player's input, then restores a saved state and re-simulates the affected frames when the real input turns out to be different. Your own input is never delayed.

Is this a real game engine or a video?

It is a real simulation. Two independent clients, two netcode stacks, a simulated link, and a deterministic integer physics step, all running live in your browser. Nothing is pre-recorded.

Does anything leave my device?

No. There is no server involved and no data is sent anywhere. The page works offline once loaded.

Why does naive mode desync?

Because it applies the peer's input on whatever frame it happens to arrive, so the two clients end up running different inputs on different frames. Once the states differ there is nothing to pull them back together.

Can I play a friend over the real internet here?

Not in this build. The point of the sandbox is to control latency, jitter and loss precisely, which you cannot do against a real peer. The netcode itself is the same code you would run over a data channel.

Which games use rollback?

It became standard in fighting games through GGPO and the rollback implementations that followed it, and the same idea shows up anywhere input latency matters more than perfect agreement on every single frame.
