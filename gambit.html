What you are actually looking at

When an engine plays a move, you normally see the move and nothing else. The interesting part — the hundreds of thousands of positions it rejected to get there — is thrown away. This page keeps it.

The panel on the right lists the moves the engine is weighing at the root of its search. Each bar is that move's score: green means it favours the engine, orange means it loses ground. The list reorders itself as the search goes deeper, because a move that looks fine two moves ahead often falls apart four moves ahead. Underneath, the principal variation is the sequence the engine expects if both sides play the best moves it can find.

How it works

Generating moves

The board is a 0x88 array: 128 squares where the off-board ones can be detected with a single bitwise and. Sliding pieces walk outward along offsets until they hit something. Moves are generated pseudo-legally, then each is played and rejected if it leaves your own king attacked. That is the simple way to handle pins and discovered checks without writing special cases for them.

Searching

The search is alpha-beta with iterative deepening. Alpha-beta is minimax that stops early: once a reply is found that refutes a move, the rest of that branch cannot change the outcome, so it is abandoned. That pruning is why the engine can look several moves ahead instead of drowning in the roughly 30 legal moves per position.

Two additions matter a lot. Quiescence search keeps looking at captures past the nominal depth, so the engine never stops counting halfway through a trade and concludes it is a piece up. Move ordering tries captures of valuable pieces by cheap pieces first, because alpha-beta prunes far more when good moves come first.

Evaluating

At the leaves, positions are scored on material plus piece-square tables — small bonuses for knights near the centre, rooks on open ranks, pawns pushing forward, and a king that wants shelter in the middlegame but activity in the endgame.

How do you know it is right?

Chess move generation is deceptively hard. En passant, castling through check, pinned pieces, promotion — each is a chance for a subtle bug that only shows up in rare positions. The standard remedy is perft: count every legal position reachable to a given depth from a known starting point, and compare against counts other people have independently published.

This engine matches the published numbers on the six standard test positions, including Kiwipete at depth 4 — 4,085,603 positions, which is exactly the kind of tactical mess that catches castling and en-passant bugs. The perft panel above runs those same tests in your browser so you do not have to take my word for it.

Why it runs in the browser at all

There is no server here. The engine is a few hundred lines of plain JavaScript running in a Web Worker, which is a background thread — that is why the board still responds while the engine is thinking, and why the animation never stutters. Load the page once and it works with your network off.

Things worth trying

  • Load Mate in 2 and set strength to Strong. Watch the score for the winning move jump to M2 the moment the search is deep enough to see it — everything else stays near zero.
  • Hang a piece on purpose. The candidate list reshuffles instantly and the capture shoots to the top.
  • Set strength to Fast and play the Tricky endgame. A shallow search misjudges it; Strong finds the right idea.
  • Run the perft test and compare the counts against the Chess Programming Wiki's published values.

Questions

Is this a real engine or an animation?

Real. Move generation, alpha-beta, quiescence search and evaluation are all written from scratch with no chess library. The numbers on screen are the scores the search is returning as it runs.

How strong is it?

Roughly club strength on the higher settings. It punishes hanging pieces and finds short tactics, but it has no opening book and no endgame tablebases. It is built to be watched, not to compete with Stockfish.

Does anything get uploaded?

No. There is no server and no account, and the page keeps working offline once loaded.

What does the evaluation number mean?

Pawns, from White's point of view. +1.0 means White is about a pawn ahead. M3 means a forced mate in three.

Why does it change its mind while thinking?

It searches one depth at a time. A move that looks strong shallowly can collapse deeper, so the ranking reshuffles as the depth climbs. Watching that happen is the point.
