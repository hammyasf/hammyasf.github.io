What you're looking at

In 1952 Alan Turing wrote a paper asking how a featureless ball of cells decides where to put stripes and spots. His answer was almost too simple: let two chemicals spread at different speeds while one turns into the other, and stable patterns fall out on their own. That idea — reaction-diffusion — is now used to explain leopard rosettes, seashell markings, fish stripes and fingerprints. Morphogen runs the Gray-Scott version of it live. Every dot you see is a real balance between a chemical being fed in and one being carried away, not a texture painted on top.

How it runs on your GPU

The field is two numbers per cell — chemical A and chemical B — stored in a buffer of hundreds of thousands of cells. Each frame a WebGPU compute shader runs once per cell in parallel on your graphics card. It reads the cell and its neighbours, computes how much of each chemical diffuses in (a discrete Laplacian), applies the reaction where two B molecules turn an A into another B, and writes the next state into a second buffer. The two buffers are swapped every step, and the grid is stepped many times between frames so the pattern evolves at a satisfying pace. Because it is compute, not a video, you can reach in at any moment: painting just raises B in a disc of cells, and the simulation takes it from there. It wraps around all four edges, so patterns are seamless and tileable.

The map is the fun part

Only two numbers really matter — how fast A is fed in and how fast B is killed off — yet a change in the third decimal place is the difference between a solid field, drifting spots, wriggling worms, a coral reef, a maze, holes, or cells that split and replicate like mitosis. The little map on the right is that whole space. The dots are the classic named regimes; click between them and you land in the blends nobody bothered to name. This is the part that turns a physics demo into something you lose ten minutes to.

Questions

Is this a real simulation or a canned animation?

Real. Morphogen integrates the Gray-Scott reaction-diffusion equations on a grid every frame, in a WebGPU compute shader on your GPU. The patterns are emergent — they are not pre-rendered and not drawn in.

What is reaction-diffusion, in one line?

Two chemicals spread at different rates while one reacts with the other; that alone is enough to break a blank surface into spots, stripes and mazes. Turing proposed it as the mechanism behind biological patterning.

What are feed and kill?

Feed is how quickly chemical A is replenished everywhere; kill is how quickly chemical B is removed. These two numbers pick which pattern you get. Tiny moves across the map jump between whole families of behaviour.

Is anything uploaded?

No. Everything runs locally on your GPU through WebGPU. Nothing leaves your device and there is no sign-up.

It says my browser can't run it. Why?

Morphogen needs WebGPU compute shaders. Those ship today in Chrome, Edge, Arc and Opera on desktop, and in recent Chrome on Android and Safari on iOS. Firefox is rolling it out. If WebGPU is missing, Morphogen says so rather than showing a dead canvas.
