What am I actually looking at?

Every dot is one line of your text. To place it, Atlas runs a real sentence-embedding model that turns the line into a list of 384 numbers — a vector that captures its meaning rather than its spelling. Lines that mean similar things get similar vectors. Those vectors live in 384 dimensions, which no human can see, so Atlas uses a technique called principal component analysis to flatten them down to the two directions that carry the most variation and draws each item at that spot. The upshot: distance on the map is roughly distance in meaning. Fruit drifts to one corner, animals to another, and a word like "bat" quietly lands between the animals and the sports gear, because the model has seen it used both ways.

Wait — this runs in my browser?

Yes, and that is the point. The model is all-MiniLM-L6-v2, the same workhorse sentence-embedding model used in a lot of production search and retrieval systems. It is loaded through Transformers.js and executed with WebAssembly right on your machine, inside a Web Worker so the page never freezes while it thinks. The weights are about 23 MB; your browser downloads them once, caches them, and from then on Atlas works with no network at all. Nothing you paste is sent anywhere. That means you can safely drop in customer feedback, private notes, survey answers, or a messy backlog and see it organised without handing it to a server.

Things worth trying

Paste a pile of open-ended survey responses and let the clusters show you the themes you would otherwise read for an hour to find. Drop in a list of product names or support tickets and use "Show near-duplicates" to catch the ones that are really the same thing worded differently. Search by meaning instead of keyword — type "something expensive" over a list of products, or "feeling nervous" over a list of emotions, and watch it light up the matches even when they share no words with your query. Or just have fun: paste fifty random nouns and see what the model thinks belongs together.

How the clustering and search work

The colour groups come from k-means run directly on the full 384-dimensional vectors, not on the flattened map, so the grouping reflects true similarity even where the 2D projection has to squash things together. Move the slider and it re-groups instantly because the embeddings are already in memory. Search does the same thing a vector database does: it embeds your query into the same space and ranks every item by cosine similarity, the angle between the two vectors. Clicking a point runs that ranking from the point itself, which is exactly the "find more like this" primitive behind recommendation and semantic search.

Is my text uploaded anywhere?

No. The model downloads once to your browser and every embedding is computed on your device. Your text never touches a server, and Atlas keeps working with no connection after the first load.

How is this different from Ctrl+F?

Ctrl+F matches letters; Atlas matches meaning. Search "sad" and it will surface "grief", "heartbroken" and "down" even though they share no letters with your query, because it compares embeddings, not spelling.

What model does it use, and how big is it?

all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a 384-dimension sentence-embedding model, quantized to about 23 MB. It is cached after the first download. No API key, no per-use cost, no sign-up.

Why do the same items sometimes sit in slightly different spots?

The embeddings are deterministic, but the 2D projection is a squash from 384 dimensions to two, so a lot of nuance has to be thrown away to draw it. Distances are approximate by design. The clustering and the search both use the full-dimensional vectors, so they stay faithful even when the picture cannot.

How many lines can I map?

A few hundred is comfortable on a normal laptop. Items are embedded in batches so the page stays responsive; after that, projection, clustering and search are instant.
