How it works

This runs Swin2SR, a Swin Transformer v2 super-resolution model, directly in your browser through Transformers.js. When you pick an image, the pixels are handed to the model on your own machine, it predicts the missing high-frequency detail, and it paints back an image at two or four times the resolution. Because the whole thing executes locally on WebGPU or WebAssembly, there is no upload, no queue, and no per-image cost. It is the same class of model that paid upscaling services run on a server, except here the server is your own tab.

Why it does not choke on large photos

Running a transformer over a full 12-megapixel photo at once would exhaust memory, so the image is cut into overlapping tiles, each tile is upscaled on its own, and the results are stitched back together with a feathered weighted blend across the overlap so no seams show. Memory stays flat no matter how big the input is, and a progress bar tracks the tiles as they finish. The output is capped at a sensible maximum on the long edge so your browser stays responsive.

Which mode to use

2× Sharp is the fast, high-quality default for cleaning up a slightly small image. 4× Max pushes small images and pixel art up to poster size. 4× Photo is tuned to remove JPEG blocking and compression noise, so reach for it on screenshots, memes, and photos that have already been saved and re-saved a few times. All three run the same way, fully on your device.

Are my images uploaded anywhere?

No. The model file downloads to your browser once, then every image is processed locally. Nothing you upscale ever touches a server, which is why it is private and works offline.

Why is the first run slow?

The first time you pick a mode, the browser downloads that model (roughly 40–130 MB) and caches it. After that it loads instantly and runs offline. WebGPU, if your browser supports it, makes the actual upscaling several times faster than WebAssembly.

Is there a size or count limit, or a watermark?

No sign-up, no watermark, and no hard limit. Very large images are tiled automatically; the practical ceiling is your device's memory. The output is capped on the long edge to keep the tab responsive.

Will it invent detail that was not there?

Like every neural upscaler, it predicts plausible detail rather than recovering true information, so it is superb for photos, art, and graphics but should not be treated as forensic evidence.
