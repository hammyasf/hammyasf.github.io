Gasless transactions are not free: what a paymaster costs

July 12, 20266 min readBy Harman Kamboj
Web3Account AbstractionGaslessPaymasters

Every product team I talk with this year wants gasless transactions. The pitch is easy to love. A new user shows up, taps one button, and something lands on chain without them owning ETH, funding a wallet, or ever reading the word gas. I built the frontends for a token swap and a cross-chain bridge, and the biggest reason people quit before finishing was the gas step. Remove that step and your numbers move.

So I am not here to talk anyone out of it. I am here to say the name oversells it. Gas does not vanish when a transaction is gasless. You moved the bill onto yourself, and a bill you forgot about is the kind that hurts.

How gasless transactions actually work

Under ERC-4337, the user does not send an ordinary transaction. They sign something called a UserOperation. A bundler picks it up, and a contract called a paymaster agrees to cover the fee. The paymaster is code you deploy, fund, and control. It reads the operation, decides whether your app is willing to pay for this particular action, and if the answer is yes, the network takes its fee from the paymaster deposit instead of the user's balance. That is the whole trick. The user pays nothing. You pay for all of it.

EIP-7702 widens this. A normal wallet address can now carry code and behave like a smart account, which I got into in what EIP-7702 changes for your frontend. More wallets can play, which is good, and more of them can be sponsored, which is the part you have to budget for.

The bill is real, and it moves with the market

Here is the piece the demo never shows. Your paymaster spends real gas, and gas is not a fixed number. On a quiet Sunday it is cheap. During a busy mint or a market panic it spikes, and it spikes exactly when your traffic spikes too. So your most expensive day and your busiest day tend to be the same day. If you sponsored a flat action for every user, you signed a check whose amount you do not control.

I think about a paymaster the way I think about a payments account, not a config flag. It has a balance, a burn rate, and people who would happily spend it for you.

The failure modes I would plan for first

  • The deposit hits zero. When a paymaster runs dry, every sponsored action fails at the same moment, not one user at a time. You want a balance alert well before empty and an auto top-up with a hard ceiling so a bug cannot refill it forever.
  • Griefing. Bots find a sponsored endpoint fast and will burn your budget on junk operations for profit or just for fun. The paymaster has to validate what it is willing to pay for, and you want off-chain checks before you ever sign.
  • Operations that never confirm. A UserOperation can sit in the mempool or get dropped, same as any transaction, but through a pipeline your users have never seen. The UI has to handle a pending state that quietly goes nowhere.
  • Simulation that lies. Paymaster validation runs against current state. State can change before the bundle lands, so an operation that passed simulation can still revert on chain, and you paid to find out.

Sponsor on purpose, not by default

The mistake I would most want a team to avoid is sponsoring everything forever. Free gas for anyone with no limit is a faucet, and faucets get drained. Pick what earns the sponsorship. The first action a new user takes is worth paying for, because that is the onboarding win you were after. Actions that make you money can cover their own gas out of the margin. Past that, put a cap per user and per window so one account cannot turn your budget into their savings.

You can also meter it. Sponsor the first few operations, then ask the user to cover gas once they are clearly real and have a funded wallet. By then the friction that would have lost them is already behind you.

What I would tell a team shipping this

Gasless transactions are worth it. The onboarding lift is real and I would ship them for the right product. Just treat the paymaster like the payments system it is. Give it a budget, a fraud surface you actively watch, monitoring on the deposit, and a written policy for what you will and will not pay for. Do that and gasless is a real edge. Skip it and you built a very elegant way to hand your treasury to strangers.

If you have shipped swaps or a wallet before, none of this is exotic. It is the same honesty about gas I wrote about in a gas-aware frontend and the same trust details that decide a token swap frontend. Account abstraction just moves who pays, and moving the bill is the part worth thinking hard about. More of my Web3 writing lives in the Web3 notes.
